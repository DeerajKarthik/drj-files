“Chai peeni hai?”—a question so small, yet so loaded, it might as well have been a thesis on human connection. It was my neighbor, the one who asks me this every other day, with the same consistency of a monk reciting prayers. It wasn’t about the chai. It never was. This wasn’t caffeine; it was a ritual. A bonding exercise. The tiny knot in the vast fabric of our everyday lives that prevents us from unraveling completely.

These questions —or variations of them rather – form the scaffolding of countless relationships in this country. “Khaana kha liya?” is less about your nutritional status and more of an affirmation that someone cares whether you exist. It’s the subcontinental equivalent of a weather chat, but with a distinctly emotional undertone. You could be standing in the ruins of your life, your career reduced to rubble, but someone asking if you’ve eaten feels like a hand tugging you gently out of the quicksand. “No? Let’s fix that.”

The art of these small, seemingly inconsequential exchanges is uniquely ours. They are often not about the words themselves but the unsaid things they carry. When someone asks “Aap theek ho?” they are not merely inquiring about your blood pressure; they are performing a wellness check on your soul. Yet, somewhere between industrialization and the efficiency of one-word texts, these interactions are fraying at the edges, much like my favorite pair of jeans that I refuse to throw away.

The new breed of humans—armed with their “seen” ticks and pre-typed “sorry, busy” responses—has no room for these conversational detours. “Wassup” now floats lifeless in chats, a placeholder that hopes for nothing more than “nm, u?” in return. No chai offers. No “arrey, room aaja, let’s talk.” Efficiency is strangling affinity one syllable at a time.

It’s no one’s fault entirely. Life, we are told, must move at breakneck speed. But in our pursuit of being faster, sharper, and on-brand at all times, we’ve lost the patience required for these filler phrases, the ones that don’t directly serve our end goals but keep our humanity afloat.

What are we replacing them with? “Brb”? “Lmk”? Can you imagine someone writing an emotional letter in the 1800s with pls rply asap stamped at the end? What’s worse is that we aren’t even apologizing for it anymore. This transactional efficiency is now aspirational. “I’m just very direct bro” people say proudly, as if kindness is the last thing holding them back from unlocking their full productivity potential.

But then, sometimes, the universe surprises you. Like the guy who sold me a Violin on OLX, signing off every message with “shukriya.” Or my cab driver who played Arijit’s hits unprompted because “aap ka mood theek ho jayega, bhaiya.” Or the stranger who held a gate open for me and said, “aap pehle jaiye,” even though it delayed him by all of four seconds. These are the tiny rebellions against a world that insists we don’t owe each other anything.

Here’s the thing: small talk, suffixes, and random, utterly impractical acts of kindness aren’t inefficiencies—they’re the soul’s R&D. They don’t just oil the machinery of human interaction; they are the machinery. The world has enough people who are efficient. Be the person who asks, “How are your parents doing?” when you run into someone from school after ten years. Be the uncle who shouts “arre wah, hero lag raha hai!” when the neighbor’s son returns from the barber with a haircut that looks like it’s been done with a ruler and a vengeance.

The truth is, we spend our lives accumulating things, experiences, even digital badges of achievement—but the most human part of us is not the things we collect; it’s the spaces we carve in the lives of others. And those spaces—those pockets of presence—are born in moments of small talk, in the questions that don’t ask for answers but simply ask for you. So, maybe the real question isn’t “Khaana Khaya?” Maybe it’s, “Are we still capable of seeing each other, really seeing each other, amidst the noise we’ve built around us?”

Because at the end of the day, nobody remembers the email that was answered in 0.03 seconds with as much emotion as a coconut. They remember the warmth of being asked, “Chai peeni hai?” when they didn’t even know they needed to hear it.
