There is a particular kind of paper I no longer read on a screen. It is not the longest or the hardest. It is the one I suspect I will have to *believe* by the end of the afternoon, which means it is the one I need to argue with first.

## The experiment

Last winter I tried something unfashionable: for one month, every paper I wanted to genuinely understand went to the printer. I kept a single soft pencil on the desk and a rule that I could not open the next paper until I had written one sentence, in my own words, at the top of the first page.

<Aside>I used a 2B pencil. Harder leads scratch the paper; softer ones smudge across the next page. This is the kind of detail that ends up mattering more than it should.</Aside>

The sentences were embarrassing at first. *This paper proves something about stability, I think.* By the third week they had verbs in them.

## What the margin does

A PDF highlighter marks that you *noticed* something. A margin note marks that you *thought* something, and thinking is costly enough that you do less of it, which is the point. Where I once highlighted six paragraphs, I now annotate two, and I can reconstruct my own argument a month later from those two.

### Friction as a feature

Software wants to remove friction. For some tasks that is right. For reading it is often wrong, because the friction is where the comprehension happens: the pause to find the pencil, the flip back to the definition on page two, the arrow drawn from a claim to its proof.

### The cost, honestly

It is slower. It uses paper. It does not search. I would not recommend it for skimming a literature review, and I still skim on a screen, gladly.

<PullQuote cite="A note in my own margin, page 7">
The reason to slow down is not virtue. It is that some ideas only fit through a narrow door.
</PullQuote>

## Two small rules that survived

1. One sentence at the top of page one, in plain words, before moving on.
2. A question mark in the margin is a complete and respectable annotation.[^1]

## A different kind of attention

What surprised me was not the understanding; it was the *quiet*. A page of paper does not notify. It does not offer a related article. For forty minutes the only thing competing with the argument was my own mind, and that turned out to be a fairer fight than I expected.

### Where I still fail

I still read on trains with a phone in my hand. I still open six tabs. The practice did not turn me into someone else. It gave me one reliable place to be the reader I would like to be, and the habit of retreating there when it matters.

## Coda

If you try this, start small: one paper, one pencil, one sentence. Notice what you do with the pause. That pause is the whole technique.

[^1]: A question mark means "I do not understand this yet and I am not going to pretend". It has saved me from citing things I had only half read.
