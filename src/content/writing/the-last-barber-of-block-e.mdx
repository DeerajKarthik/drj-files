There’s a man who still cuts hair with scissors.

Not the quick-snipping, perfunctory kind that young men wield while chewing gum and scrolling reels with their free hand. No, his scissors are heavy—silver, ornate, and blunt at the tips like they’ve seen too much of life to bother being sharp anymore. He calls them “respectful” as if the hair might file a complaint.

His shop sits between a defunct laundromat and a paan shop that sells nostalgia in foil pouches. The board reads “Hair & Honour – Since 1978”, with the ampersand long faded, leaving “Hair Honour” to stand by itself, proud and mildly confusing. Nobody calls him by name. Not even his wife, presumably. He is simply “Barber Uncle,” the uncle who has been cutting the same parting since before Instagram decided your face shape deserved a fade.

The shop smells of eucalyptus oil, old talcum, and conservative regret. Inside, two chairs face opposite directions, so you can get your hair cut while contemplating either your reflection or a calendar with Sai Baba silently judging you for every missed prayer.

His clientele is loyal and balding. They come not because he’s good—though he is, in a slow, unhurried way—but because he remembers. He remembers what their fathers looked like with hair. He remembers which boy cried in ‘94 because the blade nicked his ear. He remembers when the shop next door sold socks instead of SIM cards.

“You don’t cut hair,” he tells me once, solemnly, as if correcting a theological error. “You negotiate with it.”

This philosophy might explain why a standard trim takes forty-five minutes and includes long pauses where he just stares at your head, perhaps reflecting on how your mother must feel.

Somewhere along the line, someone brought in a trimmer. It sat on the shelf for three months, untouched, like a forbidden fruit or an Alexa in an Amish kitchen. Eventually, it was used to buzz the head of a widower entering sanyas. After that, it disappeared.

Barber Uncle believes machines are for people who’ve stopped listening. He doesn’t say it. He just looks at the plug point, now jammed with an electric mosquito repellent, and sighs.

Of course, there are newer salons nearby—places where the chairs recline and the stylists wear AirPods as if communicating with an intergalactic fashion council. These salons offer “consultations” and “scalp detox.” They ask you about your goals, like a therapist, then charge you like a lawyer. Everyone walks out looking Instagrammable, which is to say, slightly less like themselves.

But Barber Uncle never promises transformation. He offers you continuity.

Once, a teenage boy walked in, asking for a K-pop style undercut with a side taper and textured fringe. Barber Uncle simply turned to his ancient radio, tuned into Vividh Bharti, and said, “Sit.” The boy left with a neat side part and a confused sense of inner peace.

In this way, the shop resists time. Not aggressively, like a cranky old man yelling at clouds, but passively, like a stubborn rock in a river, content to be eroded at its own pace.

When I ask him if he’s planning to retire, he says, “Retire and do what? Watch TV? TV shows me things I already knew—people lie, sons disappoint, women cry, and nobody washes their hands after using public toilets.”

This is not cynicism. It’s experience, gift-wrapped in apathy. He tells me this while trimming my sideburns with the precision of a surgeon and the detachment of a judge delivering a life sentence.

There’s something sacred in that routine. In a world obsessed with disruption, Barber Uncle is an ode to the interrupted. He doesn’t pivot. He doesn’t rebrand. He doesn’t care if the algorithm approves.

Sometimes I wonder what will happen to the shop when Barber Uncle is gone. Will it become a vape store? A silent coworking café filled with wireless people typing noiseless code? Will someone paint over the board? Rename it Clipz & VibeZ?

Or will it vanish quietly, like an inside joke between generations, never explained and therefore never repeated?

Last week, while he was fixing the back of my neck—always the last stroke, like a painter signing off—he paused.

“You know,” he said, “when you cut the same hair on the same man for twenty years, it stops being hair.”

I waited for him to continue, but he just patted my shoulder, handed me a mirror, and asked, “Chalega?”

Yes, it would do. It always does.
