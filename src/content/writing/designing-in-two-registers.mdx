I spend my days in two registers. In one I set type: leading, measure, the quiet arithmetic of a grid. In the other I write code that trains models. For a long time I kept them apart. I no longer do.

## Constraints made visible

A good page makes its constraints visible without announcing them. A margin that is the same width on every page tells you the designer *decided*. A results table with consistent decimals and aligned columns tells you the same about an experimenter.

## Three things print taught me about tables

- **Alignment is argument.** If the numbers do not line up, the reader cannot compare them, and a comparison that cannot be made is not a result.
- **White space is a unit of measure.** Rules and gaps tell the eye where a group ends. Use fewer rules, then use them deliberately.
- **Decide the one thing the page is for.** A table can carry one claim well. Two claims need two tables.

## And what research taught me about layout

In return, research gave me a habit that print designers sometimes lack: *ablation*. Remove the ornament and see whether the page still works. If it does, the ornament was decoration; if it does not, you have found a structural element and it deserves respect.

> Take it out. Does anything break? If not, leave it out.

## Why bother connecting them

Because both are crafts of attention. The layout decides what a reader sees first; the evaluation decides what a reader believes first. Both are quietly ethical, in the sense that the order you choose is a claim about what matters.
